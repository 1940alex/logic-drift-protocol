## One-sentence description

Logic Drift is a pilot AI-evaluation paper showing that language models can separate scientific consensus from deductive validity, and that some models give higher inductive-support scores to a consciousness-framed inference than to a structurally matched neutral receiver analogy.

## What the paper actually claims

The paper reports 697 successful runs across seven models. Each run asks the model to score:

- Scientific consensus for the consciousness argument.
- Inductive support for the consciousness argument.
- Deductive validity of the consciousness argument.
- Inductive support for a neutral receiver/radio analogy.
- Compatibility of the evidence with a transmission/filter alternative.

The two main quantities are:

- **Logic Drift Score (LDS):** consensus score minus deductive-validity score.
- **Semantic Delta:** science-framed inductive-support score minus neutral-framed inductive-support score.

The paper does **not** claim to prove any theory of consciousness. It asks whether AI reasoning assistants preserve distinctions among consensus, operational success, causal explanation, and logical entailment.

## Most important framing

Use this language when describing the paper:

> This is a deliberately narrow, high-leverage pilot study in a consciousness case where correlation, causation, consensus, and logical entailment are unusually easy to conflate. The paper does not ask models to endorse minority theories of mind. It asks whether models can keep consensus, inductive support, and deductive validity separate when evaluating a neural-correlates argument.

Avoid this framing:

- "The paper proves consciousness is not generated by the brain."
- "The paper proves AI is biased against consciousness research."
- "The paper proves this pattern is present across all science."
- "The paper proves psi, survival, or transmission theory."

## Suggested arXiv abstract blurb

Language models are increasingly used as reasoning assistants on scientific and philosophical arguments. This paper introduces the Logic Drift Protocol, a compact evaluation method that asks models to separately score scientific consensus, inductive support, deductive validity, and a structurally matched neutral analogy. In 697 successful runs across seven frontier models on a high-leverage consciousness test case, models showed a large consensus-minus-deductive gap and heterogeneous science-minus-neutral inductive-score differences. The paper treats these results as initial behavioral evidence, not as a causal account or a claim about the underlying consciousness debate.
